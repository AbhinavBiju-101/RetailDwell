Stores know what sold and roughly how many came in. Nobody knows what happened in between.

Sales data shows that a section underperforms, not why. Each of these needs a different fix, and a till report cannot tell them apart. Pick one.

A route problem

Traffic flows around the section. {{ brand }} shows it as a dead zone: few visits and little time spent. Move it onto a main path, or fix the signage that points to it.

A visibility problem

Plenty of people pass, but dwell stays low. {{ brand }} flags it as a visibility problem or a pass-through. Raise the signage to sightline height, or use the spot for impulse items.

An offer problem

Shoppers stop and then leave. That points at price, stock or confusion rather than placement. With a point-of-sale feed this becomes a funnel; today {{ brand }} shows the stop, and you check the shelf.

Sheet 2 of 13The gap

We design for the hardest customer: someone who carries nothing and identifies nothing.

No phone signal, no cart, no app. Alone or with family, free to wander, with a real wallet and no wish to be recognised. If a store can be understood from that shopper, every extra sensor is a bonus rather than a requirement.

  • Where the body isPosition on the floor, over time.
  • How it movesSpeed, direction, stopping, turning back.
  • Who it moves withCo-movement says family or friends.
  • Where it appearsThe entrance and the checkout.

Everything {{ brand }} reports is derived from those four things.

Sheet 4 of 13Why cameras

Positions, not people. The video is never stored.

Video is a means, not the product. The camera agent reads the live stream frame by frame and keeps four numbers per detection. A week of footage that runs to hundreds of gigabytes becomes a few megabytes of coordinates.

  • No face recognition, ever.
  • Track numbers are short-lived and never matched across days.
  • No phone identifiers, which would need consent under India's DPDP Act.
  • Labels such as loiterer describe groups of visits. There are no alerts about individuals, because that would turn analytics into surveillance.

Sheet 6 of 13Pipeline

Footfall alone misleads. Visits against time spent tells you what to do.

A stop is staying within about one metre for five seconds or more, so dwell does not depend on carrying anything. Every merchandise zone lands in one of four groups, and each one calls for a different move.

Presence time (inside a zone) and engaged time (stopped in front of merchandise) are kept separate, so someone resting by the door never inflates a display's numbers.

Sheet 7 of 13Visits and dwell

The same stop means different things for different shoppers.

Attention and purchase are separate. The hidden third variable is the shopper's mission, and cameras can read it from movement alone. Averaging the two kinds of shopper hides both patterns.

Sheet 8 of 13Shopper mission

A queue is not a crowd. Throughput tells them apart.

A busy aisle full of happy browsers should not trigger a bottleneck alarm. For choke points such as the till, {{ brand }} measures how many people are waiting and how many are served each minute, then estimates the wait with Little's law: people waiting divided by people served per minute.

Little's law assumes a steady state, so treat the number as a guide during surges. Try it.

Sheet 9 of 13Queues and bottlenecks

Did the move work? Compare shares, not counts.

Most tools show a heat map. Few say whether a change helped. {{ brand }} compares the share of shoppers who stopped at a display before and after, so a busy Saturday never looks like a successful move. Suppliers who pay for display space want exactly this proof.

Results come with a likely range, and show association, not proof. The cleanest test compares days of the same type with the same promotions.

Sheet 10 of 13Layout experiments

Every flag explains itself, in words a store manager can act on.

Each zone is compared with the rest of the store, and later with its own usual pattern for that time of day. The message says what was seen and what to try.

Dead zone

Visits well below the store median and dwell well below as well. Few people come and those who do leave quickly. Relocate it, or redesign the display and signage.

Visibility problem

Visits well below the median but dwell normal. People are not seeing it. Move signage to sightline height or closer to the main path.

Pass-through

Visits above the median but dwell below. Shoppers cross it without stopping. A good spot for impulse items and promotions.

Destination

Average visits but dwell well above the median. People come specifically for it. Consider seating, demos or staff.

Hot zone

High visits and high dwell. An engaged zone. Protect its footprint.

Labels carry a confidence and describe groups of visits. A resting shopper can look like a loiterer, so nothing here ever becomes an alert about a person.

Sheet 11 of 13Diagnosis

Most computer-vision demos show overlays. We show what we measured and what we do not claim.

Accuracy is stated by zone, not by centimetre. Before a store relies on a number, it is checked against a hand-annotated clip of its own footage.

What we report

  • Dwell error per zone, in seconds and percent
  • Count error at the entrance and the checkout
  • Track ID switches per minute at the chosen frame rate
  • Journey completeness, entrance to checkout
  • Classification accuracy for shopper missions on scripted clips

What we do not claim

  • Centimetre-level positions
  • That a path caused a purchase. It is correlation
  • Certain journeys. They are probable, with a confidence figure
  • Conversion without a point-of-sale feed
  • Identity across visits or across days
